2  Data Details

2.1 What are data?

As was mentioned in the first chapter, data are observations about the world and can take many forms. Data can be collected through manual means such as surveys or interviews. It can be collected more incidentally such as through capturing publicly available comments on social media platforms. Within the digital environment, data are being captured about everything we do. For example, learning management systems collect data about the number of times a student logs in, the number of times they view a video, the amount of time they spend on the platform, and many other statistics. In short, data are everywhere and is constantly be generating.

2.2 Data and information

Data are the raw unprocessed figures, such as the GPAs for students sitting in a database. Information is data that have been organized to be put to use. The average GPA of all math majors for the fall semester is 3.1—this could be considered information.

2.3 Data characteristics

Data characteristics are the qualities of the data you would assess to understand it. You can start with these characteristics to initially start engaging with data work.

Volume
The amount of data. Some datasets are small (a class survey), while others are very large (millions of student records across states).
Variety
The many formats data can take — numbers, text, images, audio, video, and more. It can also be structured (neatly organized in tables) or unstructured (like unprocessed social media posts).
Velocity
How fast data is generated and processed. Some data is collected in real time (like sensor data or live polling), while other data is more static.
Veracity
The accuracy and trustworthiness of the data. Poor-quality or messy data can lead to misleading results.
Value
Whether data are useful. Just collecting data isn’t enough — it needs to help answer questions or solve problems.

2.6 Data access types

When working with data in higher education it is important to always understand the permissions and rules associated with it. Some data can be downloaded by the general public and utilized mostly without restriction. For other data, permissions must be granted and certain legal protections must be considered. The rules about access to data and the related restrictions shape all of the work that can be done, so it must be considered up front in the process.

2.6.1 Publicly available data

Publicly available data can typically be obtained without permission. In higher education there are many datasets that are accessible by the public but they come in two forms generally. Official public data (OPD) are yielded through rigorous validated processes to ensure it is trustworthy and reliable. Unofficial public data (UPD) are shared informally. Such data are likely to be less reliable and require careful scrutiny to understand their quality.

2.6.1.1 Official public data

Official public datasets are the products of intensive inspection and are published authoritative agencies such as the Integrated Postsecondary Data System (IPEDS). All U.S. colleges and universities that receive federal financial aid are required to report data to IPEDS annually. Data represented there come from over 6,000 institutions and includes variables such as graduation and retention rates, tuition costs, and enrollment numbers. Such standardized reporting allows for institutions to be benchmarked against other similar colleges across various important features. Some sources of relevant student data are presented in the table here.

2.6.1.2 Unofficial public data

Unofficial public datasets are also available generally but may or may not be based on a formal trustworthy process. Data like this are useful in several capacities. If they are verified and validated, they can be genuinely informative. Otherwise they can be helpful for learning, creating prototypes, and exploring.

2.7 Restricted data

Compared to publicly available data, restricted data come with obligations and rules. Often access requires special permissions.

2.7.1 Institutional data

Institutional data is internal to a university and its processes. Often this is housed on university servers or on secured cloud systems. These data are related to important aspects of a school such as enrollment numbers, budgets, and accreditation metrics. As this data is so highly sensitive, access is based on roles assigned to university stakeholders, where a faculty member may have access to different data compared to a department chair. Some example systems and the type of data they may house are presented on the table in this section.

2.7.2 Protected data

Some institutional data are both restricted and protected legally. For instance, the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act limits how student data can be disseminated, obtained, and communicated about. FERPA is applicable to any school that receives federal funding and consent to access protected data must typically be connected to an educational or legal matter. Other laws are also applicable such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPAA) for health care data if there is an on-campus clinic. Due to such restrictions, it is always important to be aware and thoroughly understand any regulations relevant to data being worked with in the execution of data science projects. Also, keep in mind the privacy and rights of the individuals represented in the data throughout your engagement with it. Always work cautiously!

2.8 Chapter 2 Exercises:

NoteExercises

  1. Classify the data around you. Think about some data in your own professional context or data from an envisioned one. Identify one of each dataset access type—a) public facing, b) internal, and c) legally protected.

    1. Describe and place. For each dataset, describe what it is, its access type, and the system type where it resides (e.g., SIS, LMS).

    2. Define characteristics. For these datasets, explain their properties with the five characteristics we discussed (volume, variety, velocity, veracity, value).

    3. Path to access. For these datasets, what types of permissions and regulations govern them?

  2. Data to information. A colleague gives you a spreadsheet of all students’ individual GPAs for the year and requests that you analyze it for helpful insights.

    1. As information. Explain two ways you might organize or summarize the raw data transforming it into insights a decision maker could make use of.

    2. Risk. One major found in the data has only three students. How might reporting on the GPA by major be a privacy concern, especially if the results were to be reported publicly? How might you address the issue?

  3. Public data source. Choose a dataset from an official public source (e.g., IPEDS) and one from an unofficial public source (e.g., Kaggle).

    1. Trustworthy?. What was the process that produced it, and what does that mean for how you could use it for your work?

    2. Define guiding questions. Create one question for each dataset that might serve to guide your analysis if you were to undertake one. Explain them and how they connect to the data.
